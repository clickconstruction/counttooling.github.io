How long does it take to count a restaurant?

The segments now answer a question. A line over the film asks it, a stopwatch counts up beside it in real time, each chapter stamps the clock as it finishes, and when the film ends the question resolves into the answer: forty-three seconds, start to bid. The Question toggle turns the line and the clock on and off; the Style row still swaps the three looks; the trade chips change the question and the third word. The film is untouched; everything drawn is an HTML layer on the video's clock, which is how it would ship.

A · Chapter bar

The four names on one line

The chapter names on one line under the film, a single track filling green beneath them, the current chapter lit and the passed ones ticked. The live beat ("Hangers, from the rulebook.") sits under the track, so the caption moves out of the frame and into the bar.

  • Reads at a glance and matches the "takeoff loop" language used everywhere else on the site.
  • One fill across four chapters of very different lengths: Connect is 16 s, Count is 6 s, so the sweep is uneven and the arrow row can't show where you are inside a chapter.
B · Story segments · recommended

One segment per chapter, each filling in turn

The story-progress idiom people already know from their phones. Each chapter is its own track, so the fill is honest about where you are inside it; the chapter names sit under the tracks with their length; the live beat sits beneath with a pulse. Every segment is a button: click Check and the film jumps there.

  • The only treatment that makes the hero browsable: a plumber can skip straight to the riser or the hand-off.
  • Fits the 980px hero and collapses cleanly on a phone (the names stay, the beat wraps).
  • Costs one array per film in index.html and about forty lines of script.
C · Ledger rail

A checklist beside the film

On a wide screen the four chapters stand as a column to the right, ticking green as the film passes them, the current one holding the live beat. It reads like the Bid Check list inside the app, which is a nice echo. Under 720px it becomes the A bar.

  • The most legible while the film plays: the eye never leaves the frame's edge.
  • Narrows the film by 250px on desktop, and the landing spec pins the hero at 980px, so the frame would need to grow or the rail would need to sit under it.
D · Ticker

A thin line and one word

The least chrome: a 3px line along the top edge of the film filling green, chapter ticks on it, and the current chapter's name riding the playhead as a small pill. Nothing below the frame changes.

  • Keeps the hero exactly the height it is; works with the baked captions left in place.
  • Too quiet to be the thing a first-time visitor notices, and the four names are never shown together.

The claim the clock makes, and keeping it honest

A stopwatch over a demo film is a promise: this is how long it takes. The films can carry it, with three conditions.

  • The clock is the film's own clock. It reads the video's current time, so it cannot drift from what the viewer is watching, and it resets when the film loops or the chip changes.
  • The films are real interactions at real speed. The generator walks a scripted takeoff frame by frame with actual mouse moves, typing at about twelve characters a second, and the app's own dialogs; nothing is sped up in the encode. That is what lets the label say "real time · no cuts". If a future film ever skips or accelerates a beat, the label has to go with it.
  • The answer names the scope. "Forty-three seconds, start to bid" is true of the film: three sheets kept from thirty, the scale proved, twelve fixtures counted, two lines run with their hangers and a riser, the check, the hand-off. It is not a claim about a full restaurant set, and the question should stay as specific as the film. The electrical and HVAC films get their own question and answer, which the chips already swap in the mock.

What building it involves

  • The end screen. The film no longer loops. It stops on its last frame, the finished takeoff with the hand-off toast, and the strip holds the answer, the four stamped times, Play again and the other two trades as the next film. That replaces the landing's current behaviour, where the three films play in turn unprompted. A chip click still swaps the film at once; Play again restarts the same one; the next-takeoff buttons are the chips by another name. Reduced-motion readers are unaffected, they keep the still.
  • Placement. Below the film keeps the app's own footer and status bar visible in the last row of pixels, which the films use (the tally, the save state). Over the film saves about 70px of hero height on a phone but covers that footer and needs a scrim; the toggle above shows both. If the bar goes over the film, the next render should crop the caption pill and shift the footer beats up.
  • An HTML layer, not a re-render. The overlay listens to the video's timeupdate and reads a chapter array per film. The films stay as they are; only the baked caption pill goes away on the next render, because the bar carries the beat now.
  • Seek on click. With B (or A with a click target per name) the chips choose the trade and the segments choose the chapter, which makes the hero the fastest tour of the app on the site.
  • Reduced motion. Readers who keep the still get the four names as a static row with nothing filling, which still tells them what the app does in four words.
  • The spec. seo.spec.js pins the hero's markup and the chips; the bar would join it as one assertion per film, plus the existing hero-video generator writing the chapter array so the timings can't go stale.
  • Phone. The bar sits under the frame at every width; the names shrink to 12px and Connect's beat wraps to two lines, both checked here at 375px.
